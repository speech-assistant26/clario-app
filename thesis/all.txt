ABSTRACT
Dysarthria is a motor speech disorder that reduces the intelligibility of spoken
language by degrading the articulation of individual speech sounds. Speech-andlanguage therapy for dysarthria depends on repeated, high-volume articulation
practice, but a clinician can only be present for a small fraction of that practice.
Existing automatic tools are of limited help: mainstream automatic speech
recognition returns a single word-level verdict, which tells a learner that a word was
unclear but not which sound failed, and conventional mispronunciation-detection
systems are trained on non-native learner speech whose error patterns differ
substantially from those of motor speech disorders.
This dissertation presents Clario, a phoneme-level pronunciation error detection
system for dysarthric and unclear speech, together with the deployable web
application built around it. The central methodological contribution is the
labelling strategy. A first attempt (Method 1) derived error labels by taking a
fixed percentile of the deviation distribution computed over all phonemes. Because
that percentile was applied globally, a fixed fraction of healthy control phonemes
was forced into the error class by construction, and the resulting model flagged
errors in clear speech at inference time. Method 2, the approach adopted in
this work, redefines the ground truth around a control baseline: every phoneme
produced by a healthy control speaker is labelled correct by definition and retained
as a positive anchor, while each dysarthric phoneme is compared against its
positionally matched control realisation of the same target word, and is labelled
as an error only when its log-mel deviation falls in the top 30% of the dysarthric
deviation distribution. The error threshold is therefore never computed over
control data, and well-produced dysarthric phonemes are correctly retained as
positive examples.
The pipeline pairs 10,000 dysarthric–control recordings from the UASpeech
corpus. Both recordings of each pair are force-aligned against the same target
phoneme sequence, obtained from the control transcript via grapheme-to-phoneme
conversion, using a wav2vec2 CTC phone recogniser and the Viterbi forcedalignment algorithm. Each aligned phoneme is cropped with 20 ms of context and
converted into a fixed 64 × 32 log-mel patch, yielding 98,306 labelled phoneme
instances (14,746 error, 83,560 correct). A CNN–BiLSTM classifier — two
convolutional blocks feeding a 128-unit bidirectional LSTM, 415,810 parameters
in total — is trained on a class-balanced training split while the test split retains
the natural class distribution.
ii

Five architectures were compared under a common protocol. CNN–BiLSTM
achieved the highest error-class recall (76.64%) and the highest error-class F1
(74.04%) at 73.13% accuracy, and was selected because recall on the error class
is the operationally important quantity for a screening tool: a missed error is
a missed opportunity for corrective feedback. CNN–BiGRU attained marginally
higher accuracy (73.40%) and precision (74.19%) but lower recall (71.75%); the
plain CNN baseline without a recurrent stage lost roughly four points of F1 ,
confirming that the temporal modelling stage contributes measurably. On the
naturally imbalanced held-out split the selected model reaches 71.99% accuracy
with 75% recall and 32% precision on the error class; the low precision under
natural imbalance is analysed in detail and attributed jointly to the class prior
and to the intrinsically noisy, distance-derived nature of the labels. Per-phoneme
analysis shows the highest predicted error rates on the alveolar flap /R/, the rhotic
schwa /Ä/, the velar nasal /ŋ/ and reduced vowels, which is consistent with the
articulatory profile of dysarthria reported in the clinical literature.
The trained model was deployed as Clario, a three-tier application comprising a
React progressive web application, a FastAPI inference service and the model
artefacts. The service reproduces the training-time feature pipeline exactly,
including the global mel normalisation constants, and adds two production
safeguards absent from the notebook: an audio quality gate that rejects clipped,
silent or noise-dominated recordings before inference, and a Whisper-based targetword verification step that distinguishes “a different word was spoken” from “the
target word was mispronounced”. Median end-to-end latency for a single-word
analysis is a few seconds on commodity CPU hardware, and the whole system
deploys as a single Docker image.
The work demonstrates that anchoring the ground truth to a healthy reference
speaker, rather than to a global statistic, is what turns a phoneme error-detection
model from a system that flags errors everywhere into one that separates impaired
from clear speech. Limitations — most notably that the labels are proxy labels
derived from acoustic distance rather than clinician annotations, and that the
validation split was reused as the test split — are stated explicitly, and a
programme of clinical validation is proposed as future work.
Keywords: dysarthric speech; phoneme-level error detection; mispronunciation
detection and diagnosis; forced alignment; wav2vec2; CNN–BiLSTM; log-mel
spectrogram; UASpeech; computer-aided pronunciation training; speech-language
pathology.

INTRODUCTION

1.1

Background

Speech is the primary channel through which most people participate in social,
educational and economic life. When the motor system that controls speech is
damaged, that channel narrows. Dysarthria is a collective term for a group of
motor speech disorders that arise from neurological injury to the motor–speech
subsystems: respiration, phonation, resonance, articulation and prosody [1]. It
is a symptom rather than a disease, and it is associated with cerebral palsy,
stroke, traumatic brain injury, Parkinson’s disease, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis
and multiple sclerosis, among other conditions. Estimates place the prevalence of
dysarthria at roughly a third of stroke survivors and the large majority of people
with advanced Parkinson’s disease [2].
The perceptual consequence of dysarthria is reduced intelligibility. A listener
hears speech that is slurred, imprecisely articulated, hypernasal, or irregular in
rate and loudness, and consequently has to work harder — and often fails — to
recover the intended message. Crucially, the loss of intelligibility is not uniform
across the utterance. It is concentrated at the level of individual speech sounds, or
phonemes: consonant clusters collapse, fricatives lose their high-frequency energy,
stops are released weakly or not at all, and vowel space is centralised so that
distinct vowels converge on a schwa-like target [3]. The clinical picture is a
phoneme-level phenomenon, and effective therapy is therefore organised around
phoneme-level targets.
Speech-and-language therapy for dysarthria is predominantly behavioural. A
speech-language pathologist (SLP) identifies which articulatory targets a client is
failing to reach, prescribes drills for those targets, and provides corrective feedback
during practice. The evidence base is consistent on one point: outcomes depend
on the volume of practice with accurate feedback [4]. This is precisely where the
model of care breaks down. Clinical contact time is scarce and expensive; in many
health systems a client sees an SLP for perhaps one hour a week, and in lowresource settings far less. The remaining practice happens at home, unsupervised,
where the person practising has no reliable way to know whether a repetition
was better or worse than the last one. Self-monitoring is itself impaired in many
1

dysarthric speakers, so the feedback loop that the therapy depends on is effectively
open.
Automating that feedback loop is an obvious target for speech technology, and
the surrounding technology has advanced dramatically. Self-supervised acoustic
models such as wav2vec 2.0 [5] learn representations from raw audio that transfer
to phone recognition with very little labelled data; large multilingual recognisers
such as Whisper [6] transcribe unconstrained speech at near-human word accuracy;
and forced-alignment algorithms can place phoneme boundaries in an utterance
to within a few tens of milliseconds. What has not followed is a tool that converts
this capability into the specific artefact a person practising at home needs: a
per-sound verdict on the word they just said.

1.2

Motivation

Three observations motivated this project.
First, word-level feedback is the wrong granularity. A conventional
recogniser presented with a dysarthric production of the word “water” either
recognises it or it does not. If it does not, the learner is told that the word
was wrong. That verdict carries almost no instructional content: it does not say
whether the initial /w/ was weak, whether the medial flap /R/ was omitted, or
whether the final rhotic /Ä/ was centralised. A therapist would name the failing
sound and prescribe a drill for it. A screening tool that cannot do the same is of
limited value in practice.
Second, existing pronunciation-assessment research targets a different
population. The mature body of work on mispronunciation detection and
diagnosis (MDD) is built around computer-aided pronunciation training for
second-language learners [7, 8].
Non-native errors are largely categorical
substitutions: a learner whose first language lacks /T/ substitutes /s/ or /t/,
producing a well-formed but incorrect phoneme. Dysarthric errors are largely
gradient distortions: the target phoneme is attempted, but the articulatory gesture
is incomplete, mistimed or imprecise, producing a token that is neither the target
nor any other clean phoneme [3]. A classifier trained to decide “which phoneme
was actually produced” is answering the wrong question for this population; the
right question is “how far did this production deviate from a clear realisation of
the intended sound”.
Third, and most consequentially for this work, defining the ground truth
is harder than training the model. Supervised phoneme-level error detection
2

requires a label for every phoneme instance, and no large corpus of clinicianannotated per-phoneme error labels for dysarthric speech is publicly available.
Labels must therefore be derived. The initial approach taken in this project derived
them by computing an acoustic deviation for every phoneme and declaring a fixed
top percentile of that distribution to be errors. This is superficially reasonable and
turned out to be badly wrong: because the percentile was applied to the pooled
distribution over both impaired and healthy speakers, a fixed fraction of healthy
control phonemes was labelled as erroneous by construction. The trained model
duly learned that roughly that fraction of any recording should be flagged, and
at inference time it reported errors throughout perfectly clear control recordings.
The system was statistically well-behaved and clinically useless. Diagnosing that
failure, and redesigning the labelling scheme around a healthy control baseline,
became the methodological core of this dissertation.

1.3

Problem Statement

People with dysarthria and other sources of unclear articulation lack an accessible,
automatic means of obtaining phoneme-level feedback on their speech during
unsupervised practice. Contemporary automatic speech recognition provides
only a word-level verdict; established mispronunciation-detection methods assume
categorical substitution errors characteristic of second-language learners rather
than the gradient distortions characteristic of motor speech disorders; and
supervised approaches are blocked by the absence of clinician-annotated perphoneme ground truth for this population. Furthermore, naive strategies for
deriving that ground truth from acoustic deviation statistics — specifically, global
percentile thresholding — systematically mislabel healthy speech as erroneous,
producing models that cannot distinguish impaired from clear production.

problem is to produce a sequence of tuples ⟨pi , tstart

1.6

Scope and Delimitations

1.6.1

In scope

• Single-word and short-phrase analysis in American English, using the en-us

• Known-target assessment: the word the speaker intends to say is supplied
to the system in advance, as is standard in reading-based therapy drills. The
system does not have to guess the target.
• Binary per-phoneme classification: each phoneme is judged correct or error.
The system reports a continuous error probability alongside the binary verdict.
• A deployable web application with recording, upload, waveform visualisation,
per-phoneme breakdown and an adjustable sensitivity threshold.

5

1.6.2

Out of scope

• Diagnosis. The system produces a screening signal about acoustic deviation.
It does not diagnose dysarthria, does not classify dysarthria subtype or severity,
and is not a medical device.
• Error typology. The system reports that a phoneme deviated, not how — it
does not distinguish substitution from distortion, omission or insertion.
• Prosody and fluency. Rate, stress, intonation and voice quality are not
assessed, although they are clinically relevant.
• Multilingual support. Only American English is supported.
• Longitudinal progress tracking. Each analysis is independent; no user
accounts or session history are maintained.
• Clinical validation. No trial with clinician-annotated ground truth or with
human participants was conducted; this is identified as the principal item of
future work.

1.7

Contributions

The specific contributions of this dissertation are as follows.
C1. A diagnosis of the global-percentile labelling failure. The dissertation documents, with reference to the concrete failure mode observed, why
applying a percentile cutoff to a pooled deviation distribution guarantees
that healthy speech is mislabelled, and why the resulting model cannot
separate clear from impaired production (Section 3.7.2).
C2. A control-baseline labelling strategy. A labelling scheme in which
every control phoneme is a guaranteed-correct anchor retained in training,
and in which the error cutoff is estimated only over the dysarthric deviation
distribution, so that control data is structurally incapable of entering the
error class (Section 3.7.3).

aligned to the same canonical phoneme sequence derived from the control
transcript, so that phoneme i of the impaired production is guaranteed to
correspond to phoneme i of the control production, which is what makes
a per-phoneme distance meaningful (Section 3.5).

a common protocol, with the selection criterion made explicit and justified

C5. A per-phoneme error profile of the trained model, compared against

C6. A deployed reference implementation, Clario, comprising a FastAPI

LITERATURE REVIEW

2.1

Introduction

This chapter surveys the work on which the present system is built. It proceeds
from the clinical characterisation of dysarthric speech (Section 2.2), through
the corpora available for research (Section 2.3), to the two technical traditions
that intersect in this project: automatic pronunciation assessment (Section 2.4)
and dysarthric speech processing (Section 2.5). It then reviews the enabling
components used in the pipeline — grapheme-to-phoneme conversion (Section 2.6),
forced alignment (Section 2.7), self-supervised representations (Section 2.8)
and the acoustic features and neural architectures employed for phoneme-level
classification (Sections 2.9–2.10). It closes with a discussion of labelling strategies
for weakly supervised speech assessment (Section 2.11), a comparative summary
(Section 2.12) and a statement of the research gap (Section 2.13).

2.2

Dysarthric Speech: Clinical and Acoustic Characterisation

Darley, Aronson and Brown’s foundational perceptual studies established the
classification of dysarthria into flaccid, spastic, ataxic, hypokinetic, hyperkinetic
and mixed types, each associated with a distinct lesion site and a distinct cluster
of perceptual deviations [9]. Duffy’s subsequent synthesis [1] remains the standard
clinical reference and frames dysarthria as a disorder of the execution of speech
movements, distinguishing it from apraxia of speech (a disorder of motor planning)
and from the language-level impairments of aphasia.
From a signal-processing perspective, four families of acoustic consequence are
consistently reported.
Vowel space compression. Kim, Hasegawa-Johnson and Perlman [3] showed on
UASpeech data that the area of the vowel quadrilateral in the F1 –F2 plane shrinks
with dysarthria severity, and that this compression correlates with intelligibility.
Acoustically distinct vowels migrate towards a central, schwa-like target, which is
why reduced vowels are difficult to discriminate in impaired speech.
8

Imprecise consonant articulation. Stop consonants are released weakly or not
at all, voice-onset time becomes irregular, and fricatives lose the high-frequency
energy that carries their identity. Consonant imprecision is routinely the single
strongest perceptual correlate of reduced intelligibility [10].
Prosodic and temporal disruption. Speaking rate is reduced, segment
durations lengthen and become more variable, and inter-word pauses increase
[11]. Segment duration in dysarthric speech is therefore an unreliable cue and a
poorly behaved feature dimension.
High within-speaker variability. Repetitions of the same word by the same
dysarthric speaker differ far more than repetitions by a control speaker. This is a
defining property of the population and it has direct methodological consequences:
any labelling scheme that assumes a stable acoustic target per speaker will be noisy,
and any model trained on such data must tolerate high intra-class variance.
The final point deserves emphasis, because it distinguishes dysarthric errors from
the errors studied in the mainstream pronunciation-assessment literature. A
second-language learner who cannot produce /T/ substitutes a different but wellformed phoneme. A dysarthric speaker attempting /T/ typically produces a token
that is a degraded version of /T/ — not a clean substitute. The distinction is
between categorical substitution and gradient distortion, and it implies that a
classifier over phoneme identity is the wrong formulation for this population; what
is required is a measure of deviation from a clear realisation of the intended target.

2.3

Corpora for Dysarthric Speech Research

Two corpora dominate English-language dysarthria research.
UASpeech [12], released by the University of Illinois Urbana–Champaign,
contains isolated-word recordings from 15 speakers with cerebral-palsy-related
dysarthria spanning a wide intelligibility range, together with 13 age-matched
control speakers. The vocabulary comprises digits, computer commands, radio
alphabet letters, common words and uncommon words, and each item is recorded
in multiple blocks through a seven-microphone array. Its decisive property for the
present work is that it is paired: for a given word, both dysarthric and healthy
control productions exist. This makes it possible to define “correct” empirically,
by reference to a healthy production of the same word, rather than by an arbitrary
statistical criterion.
TORGO [13] pairs acoustic recordings with electromagnetic articulography,
giving direct measurement of articulator positions for speakers with cerebral palsy
9

and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis. It is smaller than UASpeech and its value lies
chiefly in articulatory-inversion research; it was not adopted here because the
present method requires paired matched-word control productions at scale.
Neither corpus provides clinician-annotated per-phoneme error labels. This
absence is the single most important constraint on supervised phoneme-level work
in this domain and it is the reason the labelling strategy, rather than the classifier,
is the substantive methodological problem addressed in this dissertation.

Neural approaches reformulate MDD as sequence labelling. Leung et al. [15]

of motor speech disorders in the manner described in Section 2.2; and it requires
phone-level annotation of what was actually produced, which for dysarthric speech
is both expensive and unreliable, because trained annotators disagree substantially
when asked to transcribe distorted tokens.

2.5

Automatic Recognition and Assessment of Dysarthric
Speech

Research on dysarthric speech has concentrated on two tasks.
Recognition. The goal is to transcribe dysarthric speech accurately despite
acoustic mismatch with models trained on typical speech. Speaker adaptation,
data augmentation by tempo and formant perturbation, and severity-aware model
selection have all been shown to reduce word error rate [17, 18]. Google’s Project
Euphonia demonstrated that personalised models trained on a modest amount
of a particular speaker’s data can dramatically outperform speaker-independent
systems [19]. This literature optimises transcription accuracy, not diagnostic
feedback.
Severity and intelligibility estimation. A second line predicts an utterancelevel or speaker-level intelligibility score, typically by regression over utterance
embeddings [20]. These systems answer “how impaired is this speaker” rather
than “which sound in this word failed”. The output is a scalar, and scalars do not
tell a person practising at home what to practise.
The gap between these two lines is the space this dissertation occupies: retaining
the known target word (as in assessment) and producing a per-phoneme verdict
(as in MDD), for a population whose errors are gradient distortions rather than
categorical substitutions.

2.6

Grapheme-to-Phoneme Conversion

Deriving the canonical phoneme sequence for a target word is a prerequisite for
alignment. Approaches range from pronunciation lexicons such as CMUdict, which
11

are exact but closed-vocabulary, through joint-sequence n-gram models [21], to
neural sequence-to-sequence models [22].
This work uses espeak-ng, an open-source formant-synthesis engine whose rulebased front end emits IPA transcriptions for arbitrary input text. Three properties
motivated the choice: it is open-vocabulary, so a user may practise any word
including names and nonce words; its IPA inventory for the en-us voice was
verified to coincide with the vocabulary of the chosen alignment model, so no
symbol mapping table is required; and it is a small native binary that adds
negligible latency and memory footprint to a deployed service. Its limitation is that
it is rule-based and therefore mispronounces some irregular English orthography,
and that it emits a single canonical pronunciation without alternatives.

2.7

Self-Supervised Speech Representations

wav2vec 2.0 [5] learns speech representations by masking spans of a latent feature
sequence and solving a contrastive task over quantised targets, using unlabelled
audio only. Fine-tuned with a CTC head on a small amount of labelled data,
it approaches or exceeds fully supervised systems. HuBERT [25] and WavLM
[26] refine the pre-training objective; Whisper [6] takes the alternative route of
large-scale weakly supervised multilingual training and is notable for robustness
to accent, noise and domain shift.
Two such models are used here, in distinct roles. The multilingual phone
recogniser wav2vec2-lv-60-espeak-cv-ft [27], trained to output IPA phone
symbols, supplies the emission matrix for the forced alignment of Equation (2.2).
Separately, whisper-tiny.en is used in the deployed service purely as a targetword verifier — to distinguish “the user said a completely different word” from
“the user mispronounced the target word” — a role in which its known tendency
to normalise disordered speech towards plausible words is acceptable because only
a coarse similarity decision is required.

2.10

Neural Architectures for Speech Classification

2.10.1

Convolutional networks

Two-dimensional convolution over a time–frequency image exploits the locality
and translation equivariance of spectro-temporal patterns: formant transitions,
fricative noise bands and stop bursts are local structures whose identity is largely
independent of absolute position. Abdel-Hamid et al. [29] established the value of
convolutional layers for speech recognition, and CNNs over log-mel inputs remain
the standard front end for audio classification [30].
2.10.2

Recurrent networks

A phoneme is not a static pattern; its identity and its quality are carried by
transitions. Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks [31] model such temporal
dependence through gated memory, and Gated Recurrent Units (GRUs) [32]
achieve comparable modelling capacity with fewer parameters. Bidirectional
variants [33, 34] process the sequence in both directions, so that the representation
at each step is conditioned on both preceding and following context. For
a phoneme patch that has already been extracted in full, bidirectionality is
unambiguously appropriate: the entire segment is available at inference time, and
the release of a stop is only interpretable in the light of its closure.
2.10.3

Hybrid CNN–RNN models

The combination — convolutional layers extracting local spectro-temporal features, followed by a recurrent layer integrating them along the time axis — is the
dominant design for audio classification tasks of this shape [35], and variants of
it have been reported for dysarthric speech classification [36]. This is the family
from which the five candidates compared in Chapter 6 are drawn.
2.10.4

Transformer alternatives

Transformer encoders now dominate large-scale speech modelling. They were not
adopted for the classifier here for three reasons: the input is a very short fixedsize patch (16 time steps after pooling), a regime in which self-attention offers
14

little advantage over recurrence; the labels are proxy labels derived from acoustic
distance, so the effective supervision is noisy and a high-capacity model would
fit that noise; and the deployment target is free-tier CPU hardware, where a 0.4million-parameter recurrent model is preferable to a transformer by an order of
magnitude in latency. The pre-trained transformer capacity that the system does
use is confined to the alignment stage, where it is applied as a frozen feature
extractor.

2.11

Labelling Strategies for Weakly Supervised Speech
Assessment

Where per-token annotation is unavailable, labels must be derived. Three families
of strategy appear in the literature.
Reference-comparison methods compare a production against a reference
realisation of the same content, typically with dynamic time warping over spectral
features. This is the classical basis of pronunciation scoring in computer-aided
language learning [37], and it is the family to which the present method belongs.
Model-posterior methods such as GOP (Equation (2.1)) derive labels or scores
from an acoustic model’s own confidence.
Distribution-thresholding methods compute a continuous deviation statistic
for every token and declare a fixed proportion of the distribution erroneous. This
is attractive because it needs no reference and no acoustic model, and it guarantees
a workable class balance.
It is the third family that contains the failure mode this dissertation documents.
If a percentile cutoff is applied to a distribution pooled over both impaired and
healthy speakers, then by definition a fixed fraction of the healthy tokens is
assigned to the error class, however clear those tokens actually were. The label
noise so introduced is not random: it is systematic and concentrated exactly on the
examples that should define correctness. A model trained on such labels learns the
base rate, not the phenomenon. The control-baseline strategy of Section 3.7.3 is a
reference-comparison method designed specifically to eliminate this pathology, by
restricting the percentile estimation to the impaired sub-population and treating
the healthy sub-population as a set of guaranteed-correct anchors.

2.13

Research Gap

The review supports four observations.
1. Automatic pronunciation assessment is mature but is formulated around
categorical substitution errors in second-language learners, and its central
scoring criterion (GOP) is structurally insensitive to the gradient distortions
that characterise dysarthria.
2. Dysarthric speech processing is mature but is directed at transcription accuracy
or at scalar severity estimation, neither of which produces the per-sound
feedback that unsupervised therapy practice requires.
3. No public corpus provides clinician-annotated per-phoneme error labels for
dysarthric speech, so supervised phoneme-level detection depends on derived
labels; yet the derivation strategy is rarely scrutinised, and the most convenient
16

strategy — global percentile thresholding — systematically mislabels healthy
speech.
4. Even where research prototypes exist, they are seldom carried through to a
deployable artefact that a person could actually use for practice on a phone or
laptop.
This dissertation addresses all four: it targets dysarthric rather than L2 speech,
produces per-phoneme rather than scalar output, treats the derivation of the
ground truth as the primary methodological problem and solves it with a controlbaseline strategy, and delivers the result as a deployed application.

Dataset

3.3.1

Source corpus

The UASpeech corpus [12] was selected for the reason given in Section 2.3: it is
the largest publicly available English corpus that contains matched dysarthric
19

distribution of the corpus was used so that the log-mel deviation between a
dysarthric token and its control counterpart reflects articulatory difference rather
than differences in the recording noise floor.
3.3.2

Pairing

The unit of the experiment is a pair: one dysarthric recording and one control
recording of the same target word. A transcript index was prepared in advance,
in which every row supplies four fields: the path to the dysarthric audio, the path
to the control audio, the transcript of the dysarthric recording and the transcript
of the control recording. Ten thousand such pairs were used.
3.3.3

Choice of alignment target

A pair provides two candidate transcripts, and the choice between them is
consequential.
The transcript obtained from the dysarthric recording is a transcription of what
was actually produced — including its errors. Aligning the dysarthric audio to that
transcript would place phoneme boundaries around the sounds that were produced,
thereby concealing the very deviations the system is supposed to detect. Worse,
the two recordings of a pair would then be aligned to different symbol sequences,
and the correspondence between phoneme i of one and phoneme i of the other —
the correspondence on which the entire distance-based labelling scheme rests —
would be lost.
The transcript obtained from the control recording is a transcription of clear speech
and is therefore the best available proxy for the intended target word. It is used as
the alignment target for both recordings of the pair. Phoneme i of the dysarthric
alignment and phoneme i of the control alignment are then, by construction, two
attempts at the same intended sound, and their acoustic distance is a meaningful
measure of how far the impaired attempt fell from a clear realisation. This decision
is illustrated in Figure 3.2.

3.4

Grapheme-to-Phoneme Conversion

The target word is converted to an IPA phoneme sequence by invoking the

Three post-processing steps are applied. Primary stress (U+02C8), secondary stress
(U+02CC) and the length mark (U+02D0) are stripped, because these diacritics are
suprasegmental annotations that are not members of the alignment model’s phone
vocabulary. The remaining string is then split into individual symbols, and any
symbol not present in the alignment model’s vocabulary is discarded.
The espeak-ng binary is invoked through a subprocess rather than through the
phonemizer Python wrapper. This is a practical rather than a scientific decision:
phonemizer copies the shared library into a temporary directory before loading

the installed binary in place avoids the problem entirely, and the identical code

train–serve skew.
A start-up self-check verifies that the toolchain is intact before any data is
processed: the word “well” is converted and the result checked to be nonempty and to intersect the alignment model’s vocabulary. The check confirmed
/w/ /E/ /l/, all three symbols present in the vocabulary.

3.6

Each aligned phoneme is converted to a fixed-size log-mel patch.
Context padding. The segment is widened by 20 ms on each side. Stop

3.8

Data Splitting

The corpus was split 80%/20% into training and held-out sets with stratification
on the label, under a fixed random seed of 42.
The split precedes class balancing. An earlier iteration of the pipeline
balanced the classes by upsampling and then split, with the result that duplicated
copies of the same minority instance appeared on both sides of the split. The heldout metrics obtained under that ordering were inflated and meaningless. In the
final pipeline, the split is performed first and balancing is applied to the training
partition only; the held-out partition retains the natural 15%/85% distribution.
Balancing. Within the training partition the minority class is upsampled with
replacement to the size of the majority class, and the result is shuffled. This yields
a training set of 133,694 instances at exactly 50/50 balance, and a held-out set of
19,662 instances at the natural distribution.
A stated limitation. The held-out partition was also supplied to Keras as the
validation set for early stopping, so the reported held-out metrics are obtained on
data that participated in the stopping decision. This is a genuine methodological
weakness; it is quantified and discussed in Section 6.12 rather than concealed. A
three-way split with a separate validation partition is the correct design and is
recommended as future work.

3.10

Architecture Comparison Design

Five architectures were compared, all sharing the same convolutional front end
and differing only in the temporal-modelling stage:
1. CNN — convolutional blocks followed directly by the dense classifier (no
recurrent stage). This is the ablation that quantifies the contribution of
temporal modelling.
2. CNN–LSTM — unidirectional LSTM.
3. CNN–GRU — unidirectional GRU.
4. CNN–BiLSTM — bidirectional LSTM.
5. CNN–BiGRU — bidirectional GRU.
Selection criterion. Accuracy is an inadequate criterion for this task. Under the
natural class distribution a model that predicts “correct” unconditionally achieves
29

85% accuracy while detecting nothing. The criterion adopted is recall on the
error class, with error-class F1 as the tie-breaker, on the grounds that the two
error types carry asymmetric costs in a screening application. A false negative —
a genuinely unclear sound reported as fine — silently withholds the feedback the
tool exists to provide. A false positive — a correct sound flagged for attention
— costs the user an unnecessary repetition, which is the ordinary business of
therapy practice. The system is explicitly positioned as a screening instrument,
and screening instruments are tuned for sensitivity. The user-adjustable threshold
described in Section 4.11.3 allows this operating point to be moved by the user if
a more conservative behaviour is preferred.

Ethical Considerations

Data. All model development used the publicly released UASpeech corpus, whose
contributors gave informed consent under the original collection protocol and
whose recordings are distributed in de-identified form. No new recordings of
human participants were collected for this dissertation, and no clinical trial was
conducted.
Privacy in deployment. The deployed service processes uploaded audio in
memory and does not persist it. There are no user accounts, no session history and
no analytics on recorded content. This is a deliberate design choice for a system
whose input is a biometric signal from a potentially vulnerable population.
Framing of the output. The system’s output could be misread as a clinical
assessment. Three mitigations are applied. The interface presents a clarity score
and highlights sounds for practice, and never presents a diagnosis or a severity
label. The documentation states plainly that the system is a screening signal and
not a clinical instrument. And the sensitivity threshold is exposed to the user,
31

which makes visible the fact that the verdict is a tunable decision rather than an
objective fact about the speech.
Bias. The model was trained on the speakers present in UASpeech, whose
dysarthria arises predominantly from cerebral palsy, and on American English.
Performance on other aetiologies, other accents, other age groups and other
languages is unmeasured and should not be assumed. This is stated as a limitation
in Section 6.12 and as a priority for future work.

4.7

Neural Network Design

4.7.1

Rationale

The input is a 64 × 32 × 1 log-mel patch. Two design decisions follow from its
structure.
Convolution is applied first because the discriminative content of the patch is local
in time–frequency: a weak stop release, a missing fricative noise band or a smeared
formant transition are localised spectro-temporal events, and their significance
does not depend on where in the patch they occur. Two convolutional blocks with
3 × 3 kernels give a receptive field sufficient to span a formant transition after
pooling, while keeping the parameter count small.
A recurrent stage follows because a phoneme’s quality is carried by its trajectory.
After the convolutional blocks the tensor is 16 × 8 × 32; a TimeDistributed
flatten reinterprets this as a sequence of 16 time steps each carrying a 256dimensional feature vector, and the bidirectional LSTM integrates that sequence.
Bidirectionality is appropriate because the entire phoneme segment is available at
inference time — this is not a streaming task — and because the interpretation
of a release depends on the closure that preceded it and vice versa.

4.8

Processing Pipeline Design

Figure 4.4 traces a single analysis request through the system. Two stages exist
in the deployed pipeline that have no counterpart in the training notebook, and
both were added because a deployed system receives inputs that a curated corpus
does not.
The quality gate rejects a recording before any model is invoked if it is empty,
if its RMS level is below −40 dBFS (silence or a microphone that was not actually
capturing), or if its mean spectral flatness exceeds 0.6 (broadband noise rather
than speech). Without this gate the pipeline would happily align phonemes to
room tone and return a confident set of verdicts about nothing, which is both
misleading and a waste of compute.
Target-word verification distinguishes two situations that produce very similar
model output but require opposite feedback. If the user says an entirely different
word, the forced aligner — being constrained — will still place the target phonemes
somewhere in the audio, and the classifier will report a large number of errors.
The correct response is “you said a different word”, not “your pronunciation was
poor”. A small Whisper model transcribes the recording, both transcript and
target are passed through Whisper’s text normaliser, and the maximum similarity
41

ratio between the target and any transcribed word is compared against a threshold.
This is deliberately a coarse check: it is not a pronunciation judge, only a guard
against the wrong-word case, and its threshold is set permissively so that a heavily
distorted but genuine attempt is not rejected.

4.10

User Interface Design

The interface is a single scrolling view organised into a prompt card and a result
card (Figure 4.5). The design targets NFR-06 and NFR-07: the primary action
is reachable in one tap from the landing state, and the layout reflows to a single
column on narrow viewports.
Four decisions govern the presentation of results.
The clarity score is shown first and largest, as a radial ring, because a single
legible number is what tells the user whether the attempt was better than the last
one.
Verdicts are anchored in time on the waveform. Shading each phoneme’s
43

interval directly on the signal connects the abstract verdict to the moment in the
recording at which it applies, which is what makes the feedback actionable during
repeated practice.
Uncertainty is exposed rather than hidden. Each phoneme’s row carries a
probability meter as well as a verdict, so a marginal case at P = 0.51 is visually
distinguishable from a confident one at P = 0.95. Presenting only the binary
verdict would overstate the model’s certainty — which, given the proxy-label
limitation of Section 6.12, would be misleading.
Colour is never the sole carrier of meaning (NFR-08): every verdict is also
expressed as text and as a meter position.

4.11

Key Design Decisions and Justifications

4.11.1

Server-side inference

Decision. All model execution occurs on the server; the client performs capture,
conversion and rendering only.
Justification. The three model components are a TensorFlow graph, a PyTorch
transformer and a native binary; none has a viable in-browser equivalent that
would preserve numerical equivalence with training. Porting the classifier alone to
a browser runtime would not help, because the aligner and the G2P engine would
still be server-side. The cost is a round trip carrying tens of kilobytes, which is
negligible relative to the inference time itself.
4.11.2

Client-side format conversion

Decision. The browser converts recorded audio to 16 kHz mono PCM16 WAV
before upload.
Justification. Browsers record WebM/Opus, which libsndfile cannot decode;
the server-side fallback path requires ffmpeg, a large native dependency.
Converting on the client removes that dependency, reduces the upload size, and
guarantees the server receives exactly the format the training pipeline assumed.
4.11.3

A user-adjustable decision threshold

Decision. The error-probability threshold defaults to 0.50 and is adjustable by the
user within [0.30, 0.70], with the bounds enforced server-side.
Justification. The appropriate operating point is not a property of the model
but of the use case. A clinician screening for targets wants sensitivity and will
lower the threshold; a user practising a sound they have nearly mastered wants
precision and will raise it. Exposing the threshold also serves an honesty function
— it makes visible that the verdict is a tunable decision rather than an objective
fact. The bounds are enforced server-side so that the control cannot be pushed
into a degenerate regime where everything or nothing is flagged.
4.11.4

Baked-in normalisation constants

Decision. The global mel mean and standard deviation are resolved with a threelevel precedence: environment variables, then the dataset archive if present, then
45

constants compiled into the configuration module.
Justification. These two scalars are part of the trained model (Section 3.6).
Shipping the 54 MB dataset archive purely to recover two floating-point numbers
is wasteful, and making the service silently fall back to per-clip normalisation
when the archive is absent would produce a service that runs but is quietly less
accurate — the worst possible failure mode. Compiling the values in makes the
correct behaviour the default, while the environment-variable override preserves
the ability to retrain.
4.11.5

Graceful degradation

Decision. Optional components degrade the service rather than disabling it, and
the health endpoint reports the degraded state.
Justification. Whisper-based verification is a convenience; if it fails to load or
throws, analysis proceeds without it. Model warm-up failure at start — typically
a transient network problem while fetching the aligner — is logged and retried on
first request rather than crashing the process. The health endpoint distinguishes
ready, degraded and offline, and the client surfaces that state so a user is never
left interpreting an unexplained failure.

5.4

Inference Service Implementation

5.4.1

Structure

The service is a FastAPI application of six modules: main (routing and lifecycle),
pipeline (models and inference), audio (decoding, trimming, quality assessment,
downsampling), phonemes (G2P), config (typed settings) and schemas (response

5.4.2

Model lifecycle

The three heavy models total approximately 1.3 GB and take tens of seconds
to load. They are therefore loaded once and shared. A process-wide singleton
holds the pipeline; loading is lazy and guarded by a double-checked lock so that
concurrent first requests cannot trigger duplicate loads; and a FastAPI lifespan
hook warms the models at start-up so the first user request is not the one that pays
the cost. Warm-up failure is caught and logged rather than propagated, leaving
the service running in a degraded state that the health endpoint reports (NFR-09,
NFR-13).

normalisation exists only for the case where all three sources are absent, and it
emits a warning, because a service that silently degrades its own accuracy is worse

5.4.5

Target-word verification

Verification transcribes the recording with whisper-tiny.en, normalises both the
transcript and the target with Whisper’s own text normaliser — which reconciles
spelled-out numbers with digits, strips punctuation and folds case, so that a
target of “seven” matches a transcription of “7” — and computes the maximum
SequenceMatcher ratio between the target and any transcribed word. A match
51

is declared if that ratio meets the threshold or if either string contains the other.
The check is intentionally permissive: its purpose is to catch the wrong-word case,
and a false rejection of a genuine but heavily distorted attempt would be far more
harmful than an occasional missed detection. Any exception in this stage is caught
and analysis proceeds.
5.4.6

Analysis

The analysis method is a direct transcription of the notebook inference cell: align,
crop one mel patch per phoneme, stack, normalise, and classify the whole batch
in a single predict call. Batching matters — a five-phoneme word issues one
framework call rather than five — and the per-phoneme threshold is applied
afterwards in pure Python, which is what makes the user-adjustable threshold
free of any additional inference cost.

5.5

Implementation Challenges and Resolutions

Six problems arose that materially affected the result. They are recorded here
because several of them are the kind of defect that produces a working system
with quietly wrong numbers.
5.5.1

The phonemizer library failure

Symptom. phonemizer raised “failed to map segment from shared object” on the
training host.
Cause. The package copies the espeak shared library into a temporary directory
before loading it; the training environment mounts the temporary filesystem
noexec, so the copied library cannot be executed.
Resolution. The espeak-ng binary is invoked directly through a subprocess, which
runs the installed library in place. The same code path is used in training and in
the service, which additionally eliminates a source of train–serve skew. A start-up
self-check now converts the word “well” and fails loudly if the result is empty or
disjoint from the aligner’s vocabulary, so a broken toolchain is detected before any
data is processed rather than after hours of silently empty output.

52

5.5.2

The double-trim timebase defect

Symptom. Phoneme bands drawn on the waveform were displaced relative to the
audible sounds.
Cause. Alignment was computed on the trimmed waveform, while the displayed
waveform was loaded separately and untrimmed. The two timebases differed by
the duration of the leading silence.
Resolution. Load-and-trim was factored into a single function whose output is
the sole waveform used by alignment, cropping and display. This “trim once”
discipline is now a stated invariant of both the notebook and the service.
5.5.3

Test-set contamination through premature balancing

Symptom. Implausibly high held-out accuracy in an early iteration.
Cause. Minority-class upsampling was applied before the train/test split, so
identical duplicated instances appeared on both sides of it. The model was being
evaluated partly on data it had memorised.
Resolution. The split is performed first, with stratification, and balancing
is applied to the training partition only. The held-out partition retains the
natural 15%/85% distribution. Reported accuracy fell substantially and became
meaningful.
5.5.4

Control speech flagged as erroneous

Symptom. Clear control recordings were returned as full of errors.
Cause. Method 1’s global percentile labelling (Section 3.7.2).
Resolution. The control-baseline labelling of Method 2. This is the principal
methodological contribution of the dissertation and is treated in full in Section 3.7.3; it is listed here because it was discovered as an implementation-stage
empirical failure rather than as a theoretical objection.
5.5.5

Recurrent dropout disabling the fused kernel

Symptom. Training time per epoch roughly an order of magnitude higher than
expected on the GPU.
Cause. Specifying recurrent_dropout on the LSTM layer disqualifies it from the
fused cuDNN implementation, forcing a slow generic loop.
53

Resolution. Recurrent dropout was removed and regularisation retained through
input dropout on the recurrent layer and dropout after each convolutional block.
Epoch time returned to approximately 50 s and no loss of generalisation was
observed.
5.5.6

Browser audio format mismatch

Symptom. Recordings made in the browser could not be decoded server-side.
Cause. MediaRecorder emits WebM/Opus; libsndfile does not decode it, and
the librosa fallback path requires ffmpeg.
Resolution. Conversion was moved to the client (Web Audio API decode,
OfflineAudioContext resample to 16 kHz mono, hand-written PCM16 WAV
encoder), removing the native dependency from the server image and guaranteeing
that the server receives exactly the format training assumed.

5.6

Web Client Implementation

The client is a React 18 application built with Vite, deliberately free of a
component library or state-management framework: the interaction is a single
form and a single result view, and the entire state is six values held in the root
component.
Audio capture requests a mono stream with echo cancellation, noise suppression
and automatic gain control enabled. The last of these is a considered compromise:
gain control alters the level of the recording, which the model is sensitive to
through the global normalisation, but the alternative — recordings whose level
varies with microphone distance and is frequently low enough to trip the quality
gate — proved worse in practice.
The result view renders three coordinated presentations of the same result: the
radial clarity ring, the waveform with verdict-shaded phoneme spans drawn as
an inline SVG, and the phoneme list with per-phoneme probability meters. The
waveform is drawn from the peak-preserving decimation returned by the server
(at most 2000 points), which keeps the payload small while retaining the visual
envelope of the signal.
The application is installable as a progressive web application through a web
manifest, so a user may add it to a phone home screen. Microphone capture
requires a secure context, which is satisfied by localhost in development and by
HTTPS in deployment.
54

5.7

Deployment

Two deployment topologies are supported.
Split containers (development). docker-compose builds the backend and
an nginx-served frontend as separate services, with the model directory mounted
read-only and the model cache held in a named volume so that the aligner is
downloaded only once.
Single image (production). A multi-stage Dockerfile builds the frontend
with Node, installs the backend with CPU-only PyTorch wheels — which avoids
approximately 2 GB of CUDA payload that would never be used — optionally
pre-fetches the aligner and Whisper models into the image layer to eliminate a
first-request download, and serves both the API and the compiled static assets
from one Uvicorn process on a single origin. This topology was deployed to
a free-tier container host. Because the API and the UI share an origin, no
CORS configuration is required in production, and the HTTPS origin satisfies
the browser’s secure-context requirement for microphone access.
All decision parameters — error threshold and its bounds, quality-gate limits,
word-verification toggle and threshold, device selection, CORS origins and upload
size limit — are settable by environment variable, so operating-point changes
require a restart rather than a rebuild (NFR-12).

5.8.1

Strategy

Testing was organised in four levels: unit tests over pure functions, integration

5.8.2

Unit testing

5.8.3

6.4.1

Interpretation

The recurrent stage contributes measurably. The plain CNN, which omits
temporal modelling entirely, is the weakest model on both recall (68.40%) and
F1 (69.86%), trailing the best recurrent model by 8.2 and 4.2 percentage points
respectively. This is the ablation that justifies the hybrid design: the information
the recurrent stage adds is the evolution of the spectro-temporal features across
the patch, which is where the quality of an articulatory gesture resides.
63

Bidirectionality helps the LSTM and hurts the GRU. CNN–BiLSTM
improves on CNN–LSTM in recall (+1.02 points) and F1 (+0.26 points) at
identical accuracy, whereas CNN–BiGRU differs from CNN–GRU far more
dramatically: bidirectionality lifts GRU recall from 61.41% to 71.75%, a gain
of more than ten points. The unidirectional GRU is the clear outlier of the
study, with the highest precision-to-recall imbalance of any candidate — it is
conservative, predicting the error class rarely and being right when it does. Its low
recall makes it unsuitable for screening despite a respectable precision of 73.53%.
Accuracy does not discriminate. The top three models span 0.27 percentage
points of accuracy, which is well inside the run-to-run variation expected from
a single training run with a fixed seed. Accuracy alone would not support a
selection among them, which is the empirical justification for the recall-based
criterion adopted in Section 3.10.
6.4.2

Selection

CNN–BiLSTM was selected. It attains the highest error-class recall (76.64%)
and the highest error-class F1 (74.04%), which is exactly the criterion stated in
advance in Section 3.10. The nearest competitor on accuracy, CNN–BiGRU,
trades 4.89 points of recall for 2.58 points of precision and 0.27 points of accuracy
— an unfavourable exchange for a screening tool, in which a missed error withholds
the feedback the system exists to provide while a false alarm costs one unnecessary
repetition. CNN–LSTM is very close behind CNN–BiLSTM and would be a
reasonable alternative if inference cost mattered more; it does not, at this model
size.

The figures in Table 6.4 come from the comparative study, in which all five

6.6

Analysis of Precision Under Class Imbalance

The error-class precision of 0.32 is the weakest number in this study and requires an
honest account. It means that roughly two of every three phonemes the deployed
65

model flags are, according to the derived labels, not errors.
6.6.1

The class prior accounts for most of it

Precision is a function of the base rate. The model produces 4,771 false positives
out of 16,713 true-correct instances — a false-positive rate of 28.5%. On a balanced
evaluation set with 2,949 instances of each class, that same false-positive rate
would yield 2,949 × 0.285 ≈ 841 false positives against 2,212 true positives, for a
precision of approximately 0.72 — which is consistent with the 71.61% reported for
CNN–BiLSTM in Table 6.4. The classifier’s discriminative behaviour is therefore
unchanged between the two protocols; what changes is the prior. When 85% of
instances are negative, even a moderate false-positive rate produces more false
positives than there are true positives available.
6.6.2

Label noise accounts for part of it

The remainder is attributable to the labels themselves. The ground truth is derived
from a threshold at τ = 1.20 dB on a continuous deviation measure. A phoneme
at D = 1.19 dB is labelled correct and one at D = 1.21 dB is labelled an error,
although they are acoustically indistinguishable. Some proportion of the 4,771
“false positives” are therefore instances that deviate substantially but fall just
below the cutoff. Because there is no clinician annotation available, this proportion
cannot be measured, and no claim is made about it beyond the observation that
the true precision against clinical ground truth is bounded below by the measured
value rather than equal to it.
6.6.3

Why this operating point was nevertheless accepted

Three considerations justify accepting the trade-off.
First, the criterion was fixed in advance (Section 3.10) and the asymmetry of costs
is real: a false negative silently withholds feedback, whereas a false positive costs
one unnecessary repetition of a sound the user was already practising.
Second, the operating point is user-adjustable. The threshold slider moves the
decision boundary within [0.30, 0.70], so a user or clinician who prefers precision
to sensitivity can obtain it without retraining.
Third — and most importantly — the system-level quantity that matters is not
per-phoneme precision on a distance-derived label but whether the tool separates

66

clear from impaired speech at the level of a whole recording. That is the subject
of Section 6.8, and it is the property that distinguishes Method 2 from Method 1.

6.7.1

Clinical plausibility of the profile

The ranking was not designed and is not an output the model was optimised for;
it is therefore usable as an external check on what the model has learned. Four
patterns in Table 6.6 align with the dysarthria literature reviewed in Section 2.2.
Rapid, ballistic gestures rank highest. The alveolar flap /R/ is the single
most error-prone symbol at 50.84%. A flap is a very brief ballistic contact of the
tongue tip, typically under 30 ms, and it is exactly the class of movement that
impaired motor control cannot execute reliably. Its position at the top of the
ranking is the most articulatorily specific prediction the model makes.
Rhotics and reduced vowels rank high. The rhotic schwa /Ä/ (49.71%),
schwa /@/ (45.58%) and the near-close vowels /U/ (44.30%) and /I/ (40.05%) all
appear in the top twelve, and the approximant /ô/ appears at 39.11%. Rhotics
require a precise and unusual tongue configuration and are late-acquired even
in typical development; reduced and near-close vowels sit near the centre of the
vowel space, and vowel-space compression [3] moves impaired productions towards
precisely that region, which makes them acoustically unstable.
Fricatives are over-represented. /z/ (44.09%), /T/ (43.77%), /v/ (43.46%)
and /Z/ (39.59%) all appear despite being individually less frequent than the stops
and nasals. Fricatives require sustained precise constriction to generate turbulent
noise, and reduced airflow control degrades them severely [10].
Nasals appear where velopharyngeal control is implicated. /ŋ/ (46.44%),
/n/ (40.88%) and /m/ (38.28%) all rank in the top fifteen. Hypernasality arising
from incomplete velopharyngeal closure is a cardinal feature of several dysarthria
68

types, and the velar /ŋ/ — requiring the most posterior tongue contact of the
three — ranks highest among them.
That an ordering derived purely from a data-driven acoustic distance measure
should reproduce these four clinically documented patterns is meaningful evidence
that the model has learned articulatory difficulty rather than a dataset artefact.
It is not, however, evidence that the model is clinically accurate on any individual
phoneme instance; the profile is an aggregate property.
6.7.2

A caveat on the interpretation of absolute rates

The rates in Table 6.6 are computed over the full corpus, which by construction
is half dysarthric. They are therefore not estimates of how often a given phoneme
is mispronounced in the population; they are estimates of how often this model
flags that phoneme in a corpus of this composition. The ranking is informative;
the absolute values are not transferable.

6.8

Control versus Dysarthric Separation

This is the evaluation that answers RQ2 and that motivated the entire labelling
redesign.
The failure mode under Method 1 was qualitative and unambiguous: control
recordings, produced by healthy speakers with no articulation impairment, were
returned with a large fraction of their phonemes flagged as errors. This is
mechanically explained by the labelling scheme — a model trained on labels
in which roughly 30% of all instances including control instances are marked
erroneous will learn to flag roughly that proportion of any input, and will do so
regardless of who is speaking.
Under Method 2 the behaviour is qualitatively different. Control recordings return
few or no flagged phonemes, while dysarthric recordings of the same words return
substantially more, and the difference is visible in a single-example comparison as
well as in aggregate. Section 6.9 reproduces a representative control result in full.
The mechanism is direct. Because Equation (3.4) labels all 49,153 control instances
as correct and retains them in training, the model’s notion of correctness is
anchored to a large body of actual healthy speech. Because the cutoff τ is
estimated over the dysarthric distribution only, no control instance contributes
a positive example to the error class. The model therefore learns “error means
deviating from clear speech” rather than “error means being in the upper tail of a
69

pooled distribution”, and the two definitions behave completely differently when
the input is clear speech.
This is the central result of the dissertation. The classifier architecture,
the features and the training protocol were essentially unchanged between the two
methods; only the definition of the ground truth changed, and that change is what
turned an unusable model into a usable one.

6.11

Discussion

6.11.1

The labelling strategy dominates the architecture

The comparative study spans 3.75 percentage points of accuracy and 7.11 points
of error F1 across five architectures — a meaningful but modest range. The change
from Method 1 to Method 2 changed the system from one that flagged errors in
healthy speech to one that does not, which is a qualitative change of behaviour
that no architecture in Table 6.4 could have delivered on Method 1 labels. Where
ground truth must be derived, the derivation is the more consequential design
decision, and it deserves more scrutiny than it usually receives.
6.11.2

Accuracy is the wrong headline metric

A trivial classifier predicting “correct” unconditionally scores 85.0% accuracy
on the held-out partition — 13 points above the model reported here — while
detecting nothing at all. Every accuracy figure in this dissertation should be read
against that baseline. The metrics that carry information about the task are errorclass recall and F1 , and the metric that carries information about the system is
the control-versus-dysarthric separation of Section 6.8.
6.11.3

What “error” means in this system

The model detects acoustic deviation from clear production of the intended
phoneme. That is a screening signal and not a clinical judgement, and the
distinction should be preserved in every use of the output. Three consequences
follow. The system cannot distinguish a substitution from a distortion or an
omission. It cannot distinguish speech-motor deviation from recording-condition
deviation, so an unusual microphone or a reverberant room may raise error rates.
And it does not measure intelligibility, which depends on listener familiarity,
context and redundancy as well as on articulation.
6.11.4

Comparison with related work

Direct numerical comparison with the MDD literature is not meaningful, because
that literature evaluates on non-native learner corpora with human-annotated
phone-level ground truth, whereas this system evaluates on dysarthric speech
with distance-derived labels. What can be compared is capability. The systems
surveyed in Table 2.1 either produce word-level or utterance-level output on
72

disordered speech, or produce phone-level output on non-native speech. This
work produces phone-level output on disordered speech, which is the combination
that the review found to be unoccupied.

6.12

Limitations

The following limitations bound the interpretation of every result in this chapter.
L1 — Proxy labels. The ground truth is derived from an acoustic distance
measure and a percentile threshold, not from clinician annotation. All reported
metrics measure agreement with that derived signal. The system’s agreement with
expert human judgement is unmeasured.
L2 — Validation and test partitions coincide. The held-out partition was
supplied to Keras as the validation set for early stopping, so the epoch selected
was the one that performed best on the same data on which the reported metrics
were computed. The headline accuracy of 71.99% is therefore mildly optimistic.
A three-way split is the correct design and is the first item of future work.
L3 — Low precision under natural imbalance. At the default operating
point roughly two thirds of flagged phonemes are, by the derived labels, not errors
(Section 6.6). Users must be told that flags are suggestions for attention, not
confirmed faults.
L4 — Population and language. Training data comprises UASpeech speakers,
whose dysarthria arises predominantly from cerebral palsy, speaking American
English isolated words. Generalisation to other aetiologies, other severities, other
accents, other age groups, connected speech and other languages is untested.
L5 — Alignment always succeeds. Forced alignment is constrained and
therefore never declines to place a phoneme. A sound the speaker omitted entirely
will still receive a span, and the classifier will judge whatever audio falls in it. The
alignment confidence score is returned so this condition can be detected, but the
system does not currently act on it.
L6 — Single training run per architecture. Each candidate was trained
once with a fixed seed. No variance estimates are available, so small differences in
Table 6.4 — particularly the 0.27-point accuracy spread among the top three —
should not be treated as significant.
L7 — Recording conditions. The model was trained on studio-quality noisereduced recordings and is deployed against consumer microphones in uncontrolled

73

environments. The quality gate rejects the worst cases, but a systematic domain
gap remains and its magnitude is unquantified.
L8 — No user study. No trial with speakers with dysarthria, and no evaluation
with speech-language pathologists, was conducted. Usability, comprehensibility of
the feedback and any therapeutic benefit are unevaluated.

CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK

7.1

Introduction

This chapter concludes the dissertation. Section 7.2 summarises the work;
Section 7.3 answers each research question against the evidence presented;
Section 7.4 restates the contributions; Section 7.5 discusses the implications;
Section 7.6 reflects critically on the process; Section 7.7 sets out future work;
and Section 7.8 closes.

7.2

Summary of the Work

This dissertation set out to give a person with dysarthric or otherwise unclear
speech the kind of feedback a therapist gives: not “that word was unclear” but
“that sound was unclear”. The review in Chapter 2 established that the two mature
technical traditions adjacent to this goal — mispronunciation detection for secondlanguage learners, and recognition and severity estimation for disordered speech
— each supply one half of what is required and neither supplies both, and that
the binding constraint is the absence of clinician-annotated per-phoneme ground
truth.
Chapter 3 addressed that constraint by deriving labels from the paired structure
of the UASpeech corpus. Both recordings of a pair are force-aligned to the same
canonical phoneme sequence obtained from the control transcript, so that phoneme
i of the impaired production and phoneme i of the healthy production are two
attempts at the same intended sound and their log-mel distance is meaningful. A
first labelling strategy that thresholded the pooled deviation distribution at a fixed
percentile was implemented, found to mislabel healthy speech by construction, and
replaced by the control-baseline strategy in which control instances are correct by
definition and the error cutoff is estimated over the dysarthric distribution alone.
Chapters 4 and 5 designed and built the system around the model: a CNN–
BiLSTM classifier of 415,810 parameters, a FastAPI inference service that
reproduces the training feature pipeline exactly and adds a quality gate and a
target-word verifier, and a React progressive web application that records, submits
and visualises.
75

Chapter 6 evaluated the result. Five architectures were compared; CNN–BiLSTM
was selected on error-class recall and F1 ; the selected model’s behaviour was
analysed in detail, including a frank account of its low precision under natural
class imbalance; the per-phoneme error profile was shown to reproduce four
independently documented clinical patterns; and the control-versus-dysarthric
separation — the property the labelling redesign was built to deliver — was
confirmed.

7.3

Answers to the Research Questions

RQ1 — How can phoneme-level labels be derived without clinician
annotation, and why does global percentile labelling fail?
Labels can be derived by reference comparison: aligning a healthy and an impaired
production of the same word to the same canonical phoneme sequence and
measuring the per-phoneme log-mel deviation between corresponding segments
(Equation (3.2)). Global percentile labelling then fails for a structural reason.
Applying a percentile cutoff to the pooled distribution makes the criterion “is
among the most deviant ρ% of everything” rather than “deviates from healthy
speech”. A fixed fraction of healthy control phonemes necessarily exceeds that
cutoff and is labelled erroneous. The resulting label noise is not random but
systematic, and it is concentrated precisely on the examples that ought to define
the correct class, so the model learns the base rate rather than the phenomenon
(Sections 3.7.2 and 6.8).
RQ2 — Does control-baseline labelling improve separation between
clear and impaired speech?
Yes, and the improvement is qualitative rather than incremental. Under Method 1
healthy control recordings were returned with a large fraction of their phonemes
flagged; under Method 2 they return few or none, while dysarthric recordings of
the same words return substantially more (Sections 6.8 and 6.9). The mechanism
is explicit in Equation (3.4): all 49,153 control instances are labelled correct
unconditionally and retained in training as anchors, and the cutoff is estimated
over the dysarthric distribution alone, so no control instance can enter the error
class by any route. Since the architecture, features and training protocol were
essentially unchanged between the two methods, the change in behaviour is
attributable to the labelling strategy alone.
76

RQ3 — Which architecture performs best, and by what criterion?
CNN–BiLSTM, selected on error-class recall (76.64%) with error-class F1
(74.04%) as tie-breaker; both are the best values in the study (Table 6.4). The
criterion matters as much as the winner. Accuracy does not discriminate — the
top three models span 0.27 percentage points, which is inside run-to-run variation
— and accuracy is in any case misleading under 85% class imbalance, where a
constant predictor scores 85%. Recall on the error class is the operationally correct
criterion because the costs are asymmetric: a false negative silently withholds
the feedback the system exists to provide, whereas a false positive costs one
unnecessary repetition. The ablation is informative in its own right: removing the
recurrent stage entirely costs 8.2 points of recall and 4.2 points of F1 , confirming
that temporal modelling of the phoneme’s trajectory contributes measurably.
RQ4 — Which phonemes are most frequently flagged, and is the
distribution clinically plausible?
The highest predicted error rates fall on the alveolar flap /R/ (50.84%), the rhotic
schwa /Ä/ (49.71%), the velar nasal /ŋ/ (46.44%) and the reduced vowel /@/
(45.58%) (Table 6.6). The distribution is clinically plausible on four independent
counts: rapid ballistic gestures rank highest, rhotics and centralised vowels rank
high (consistent with vowel space compression), fricatives are over-represented
relative to their frequency (consistent with impaired airflow control), and nasals
appear where velopharyngeal control is implicated. That an ordering derived
from a purely data-driven acoustic distance measure reproduces these documented
patterns is evidence that the model has learned articulatory difficulty rather than
a dataset artefact (Section 6.7).
RQ5 — Can it be deployed accessibly on commodity hardware?
Yes. The system runs as a single Docker image on a 2-vCPU CPU-only host
with no GPU, serving both the API and the compiled web client from one origin.
Cold start is 10–30 s, paid once at process start and absorbed by the warm-up
hook; warm single-word analysis completes in a few seconds, dominated by the
wav2vec2 alignment pass rather than by the classifier. The client is a progressive
web application installable on a phone home screen (Sections 5.7 and 6.10).

7.4

Contributions Restated

C1. A documented diagnosis of the global-percentile labelling failure for paired
impaired/healthy speech corpora, with the mechanism made explicit.
C2. The control-baseline labelling strategy of Equation (3.4), in which healthy
productions are correct by definition and retained as anchors, and the error
cutoff is estimated over the impaired sub-population only.
C3. The matched-pair alignment procedure that aligns both recordings to
the same control-derived canonical sequence, which is what makes a perphoneme distance well defined.
C4. A controlled five-architecture comparison with the selection criterion
stated in advance and justified by the asymmetric costs of a screening
application.
C5. A per-phoneme error profile validated against four independently documented clinical patterns.
C6. Clario: a deployed reference implementation including a train–serve parity
test, an audio quality gate and a target-word verifier — production
concerns absent from the research pipeline.

7.5

Implications

For research on weakly supervised speech assessment. Where ground
truth must be derived rather than annotated, the derivation deserves at least the
scrutiny normally given to the model. This work found a 3.75-point accuracy
spread across five architectures and a qualitative behavioural change from one
labelling decision. Papers reporting derived labels should state the derivation rule
explicitly and should test the resulting model on data that the rule implicitly
defines as negative — here, healthy speech — because that is where a labelling
pathology becomes visible.
For clinical practice. The result is not a diagnostic instrument and must not be
presented as one. What it plausibly supports is between-session practice: a client
can obtain per-sound feedback during unsupervised repetition, and a clinician
can review which sounds are consistently flagged. The interface decisions of
Section 4.10 — exposing the probability, exposing the threshold, never presenting

78

a diagnosis — are how that framing is enforced in the artefact rather than only
in the documentation.
For accessible speech technology. The system runs on free-tier CPU hardware
in a browser. The engineering choices that made this possible — a small
classifier, client-side format conversion, CPU-only dependency wheels, baked-in
normalisation constants — are transferable to other assistive speech applications,
where deployment cost is frequently the barrier between a research result and a
usable tool.

7.6

Critical Reflection

Three judgements would be made differently with hindsight.
The validation and test partitions should never have coincided (limitation
L2). Supplying the held-out partition to Keras as the validation set was expedient
and is a genuine methodological defect; the reported 71.99% is mildly optimistic
as a result. A three-way split costs almost nothing and would have removed an
avoidable caveat from every number in Chapter 6.
Each architecture should have been trained more than once. With a
single run per candidate and a 0.27-point accuracy spread across the top three,
the comparison cannot support fine distinctions. The recall differences are large
enough to be believable, but the study would be stronger with a mean and a
standard deviation over several seeds.
A small clinician-annotated validation set would have been worth its
cost. Even one or two hundred phoneme instances judged by a speech-language
pathologist would convert the central caveat of this work — that all metrics
measure agreement with a derived proxy — from an unbounded limitation into a
measured one. This is the highest-value item of future work per unit of effort.
Against these, the decision to treat the labelling failure as the central research
problem rather than as a bug to be patched was correct, and it is what gives the
dissertation a result worth reporting.

7.7

Future Work

7.7.1

Immediate

1. Three-way split. Separate validation and test partitions, and re-report all
metrics on genuinely held-out data (addresses L2).
79

2. Repeated runs. Train each architecture over several seeds and report means
and standard deviations (addresses L6).
3. Threshold characterisation. Compute the full precision–recall and ROC
curves over the threshold range and publish the operating characteristic, so
that the sensitivity slider is annotated with its actual consequences rather than
a bare number.
4. Act on alignment confidence. Treat a very low alignment score as
a candidate omission and report it as a distinct verdict from a distortion
(addresses L5).
7.7.2

Medium term

5. Clinician-annotated validation. Have speech-language pathologists annotate a sample of phoneme instances and measure the model’s agreement with
expert judgement, along with inter-annotator agreement as an upper bound
(addresses L1).
6. Error typology. Extend the binary verdict to distinguish substitution,
distortion and omission, which is what makes feedback prescriptive rather than
merely diagnostic.
7. Speaker adaptation. Calibrate the decision threshold, or fine-tune the final
layers, on a small enrolment sample from an individual user, following the
personalisation results reported for disordered-speech recognition [19].
8. Connected speech. Extend from isolated words to phrases and sentences,
which introduces coarticulation, and to prosodic assessment.
9. Domain robustness. Quantify and reduce the gap between studio-quality
training recordings and consumer microphones through augmentation with
noise, reverberation and codec artefacts (addresses L7).
7.7.3

Longer term

10. Longitudinal tracking. Persist analyses per user, with consent, and surface
progress over time on specific phoneme targets — the single most requested
capability in therapy contexts and the one that would most change the tool’s
role from screening to intervention support.

80

11. Prescriptive feedback. Map a flagged phoneme onto a targeted exercise and
a plain-language articulatory instruction, closing the loop from detection to
practice.
12. Multilingual extension. espeak-ng and the wav2vec2 aligner are both
multilingual; the classifier is the language-specific component, and cross-lingual
transfer of the control-baseline labelling strategy to a paired corpus in another
language is a direct test of the method’s generality.
13. Clinical study. A trial with speakers with dysarthria measuring usability,
comprehensibility and any effect on practice volume or outcome, which is the
only way to establish whether the tool delivers the benefit it is designed for
(addresses L8).

7.8

Closing Remarks

The most instructive outcome of this project was not the model. It was the
discovery that a labelling rule which is statistically unobjectionable — “call the
most deviant thirty per cent errors” — can be quietly and completely wrong, and
that the wrongness is invisible in the training metrics and obvious the moment
the system is shown a healthy speaker. The fix required no new architecture and
no additional data: it required deciding that correct means like a healthy speaker
producing this sound, and then enforcing that definition in the code.
Clario is a research prototype and its limitations are stated plainly in Section 6.12.
It does not diagnose, it has not been validated against clinicians, and roughly two
of every three sounds it flags may not be faults. What it does do is take a
recording of a single word and say which sound, at which moment, deviated from
clear speech — which is a thing a person practising alone at home currently has
no way to find out. Making that information available, on a phone, at no cost,
seems a worthwhile place for the work to have arrived.

CPU-only PyTorch wheels, optionally pre-fetches the aligner and Whisper models
into an image layer, and serves both the API and the compiled static assets from
one Uvicorn process on port 8000. Because the API and the UI share an origin,
no CORS configuration is required, and the HTTPS origin satisfies the browser’s
secure-context requirement for microphone access.
